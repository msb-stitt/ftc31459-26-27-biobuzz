142 what’s happening: turning the stick into the robot’s frame¶

Here you learn how cos and sin find the sides of a triangle from one angle. Then you use them to turn the stick’s arrow on the field into the robot’s own forward and strafe. In L140, Math.cos(heading) and Math.sin(heading) do this. That is how the robot drives where the stick points, whichever way it faces.

This page takes the three lines you typed in L140 one at a time. It uses the arrows of E141. We follow one example all the way: the robot faces 60°, and the stick asks for 0.64 along the field’s x and 0.16 along its y.

double heading = follower.pose().heading();

Pedro keeps the robot’s heading in radians. Draw a circle with a radius of 1. A radian is a distance of 1 around its edge. 60° is 1.05 radians, so heading holds 1.05.

Each point on that circle has an x and a y. At 60°, x is cos 60°, which is 0.50, and y is sin 60°, which is 0.87. Math.cos and Math.sin turn an angle into those two numbers.

The angle can be any size. Past 90°, the point is left of the middle, so its cos is below 0. Below the x axis, its sin is below 0. A negative angle turns the other way, clockwise.

Math.cos and Math.sin take an angle in radians. People mostly talk in degrees, as this page does. Before you hand an angle to Math.cos, check which unit it is in. Pedro’s heading is already in radians.

The same idea finds the sides of any triangle with a square corner. Label the angle you use. When the long side is 1, the side next to that angle is its cos, and the side across from it is its sin.

Make the long side k times as long, and every side grows k times. The angle stays the same. Triangles with the same angles at different sizes are called similar triangles. So the side next to the angle is k × cos θ, and the side across from it is k × sin θ. The k only scales the length. The cos and the sin come from the angle.

Turning or flipping a triangle does not change its sides. Find the angle you know. The side next to it is k × cos θ, and the side across from it is k × sin θ, whichever way the triangle points.

The robot’s forward points the way its nose does. At heading 60°, that is the arrow (cos 60°, sin 60°) on the field. Its left is a quarter turn more, (-sin 60°, cos 60°). Each is 1 long. The grid is the field’s x and y, moved to the robot’s center.

Why is left’s angle 60° at field y? Forward’s triangle has its 60° at field x.

Left’s triangle is forward’s, turned a quarter turn left. Turning keeps every angle and every side, so the 60° lands at field y. The side along field y is cos 60°, and the side along field x is sin 60°, going the negative way.

The stick says where to go on the field. After L060’s squaring, fieldXSpeed is 0.64 and fieldYSpeed is 0.16, so the stick asks for the arrow (0.64, 0.16) on the field’s grid.

From the Red Alliance Area, that arrow points 0.64 away and 0.16 to the left.

double robotForwardXSpeed = fieldXSpeed * Math.cos(heading) + fieldYSpeed * Math.sin(heading);
double robotStrafeYSpeed = -fieldXSpeed * Math.sin(heading) + fieldYSpeed * Math.cos(heading);

The robot can’t drive along the field’s x. It drives along its own forward and its own left. So we break the field’s (0.64, 0) into a piece along each. It is forward’s 60° triangle with k = 0.64: 0.64 × cos 60° = 0.32 forward, and -0.64 × sin 60° = -0.55 left. End to end, they reach the same tip. Now the grid is the robot’s.

We do the same for (0, 0.16). It is left’s triangle, its 60° at field y, with k = 0.16: 0.16 × sin 60° = 0.14 forward, and 0.16 × cos 60° = 0.08 left. This picture is drawn 3 times as large, grid and all, so the pieces show.

Now there are four pieces. End to end, they reach the stick’s tip. Arrows add in any order, so we swap the middle two.

Each number came from fieldXSpeed or fieldYSpeed and the heading. 0.32 is fieldXSpeed × cos θ, where θ is the heading.

Both forward pieces come first now, then both left pieces. They still reach the same tip.

The same four pieces, each with what makes it:

Add the forward pieces: 0.32 + 0.14 = 0.46. That is robotForwardXSpeed. Add the left pieces: -0.55 + 0.08 = -0.47. That is robotStrafeYSpeed. The robot drives (0.46, -0.47) in its own frame, and goes where the stick points on the field.

Write each sum with what makes it, in place of its numbers, and you have the two lines.

drivetrain.sticks(robotForwardXSpeed, robotStrafeYSpeed, turnCcwSpeed);

The drivetrain gets the robot’s own forward and strafe, and drives them. E143 shows where the field’s x and y are from each Alliance Area.
