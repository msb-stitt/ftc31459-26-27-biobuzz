# 142 what's happening: turning the stick into the robot's frame

Here you learn how `cos` and `sin` find the sides of a triangle from one angle. Then you use them
to turn the stick's arrow on the field into the robot's own forward and strafe. In L140,
`Math.cos(heading)` and `Math.sin(heading)` do this. That is how the robot drives where the stick
points, whichever way it faces.

This page takes the three lines you typed in [L140](l140.md) one at a time. It uses the arrows of
[E141](e141.md). We follow one example all the way: the robot faces 60°, and the stick asks for
0.64 along the field's x and 0.16 along its y.

```{code-block} java
:caption: base/HeadingHold.java
double heading = follower.pose().heading();
```

Pedro keeps the robot's heading in radians. Draw a circle with a radius of 1. A radian is a
distance of 1 around its edge. 60° is 1.05 radians, so `heading` holds 1.05.

Each point on that circle has an x and a y. At 60°, x is cos 60°, which is 0.50, and y is
sin 60°, which is 0.87. `Math.cos` and `Math.sin` turn an angle into those two numbers.

:::{admonition} fig-e142-unit-circle
:class: pencil

:::

The angle can be any size. Past 90°, the point is left of the middle, so its cos is below 0.
Below the x axis, its sin is below 0. A negative angle turns the other way, clockwise.

:::{admonition} fig-e142-quadrants
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e142-quadrants`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

`Math.cos` and `Math.sin` take an angle in radians. People mostly talk in degrees, as this
page does. Before you hand an angle to `Math.cos`, check which unit it is in. Pedro's heading is
already in radians.

The same idea finds the sides of any triangle with a square corner. Label the angle you use.
When the long side is 1, the side next to that angle is its cos, and the side across from it is
its sin.

:::{admonition} fig-e142-unit-triangles
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e142-unit-triangles`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Make the long side k times as long, and every side grows k times. The angle stays the same.
Triangles with the same angles at different sizes are called **similar triangles**. So the side
next to the angle is k × cos θ, and the side across from it is k × sin θ. The k only scales the
length. The cos and the sin come from the angle.

:::{admonition} fig-e142-similar
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e142-similar`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Turning or flipping a triangle does not change its sides. Find the angle you know. The side next
to it is k × cos θ, and the side across from it is k × sin θ, whichever way the triangle points.

:::

(cos 60°, sin 60°) on the field. Its left is a quarter turn more, (-sin 60°, cos 60°). Each is
1 long. The grid is the field's x and y, moved to the robot's center.

```{code-block} java
:caption: mytry/L140FieldRelativeOpMode.java
double heading_rad = follower.pose().heading();
double sinHeading = Math.sin(heading_rad);
double cosHeading = Math.cos(heading_rad);
double robotForwardXSpeed = fieldXSpeed * cosHeading + fieldYSpeed * sinHeading;
double robotStrafeYSpeed = -fieldXSpeed * sinHeading + fieldYSpeed * cosHeading;
```

The robot can't drive along the field's x. It drives along its own forward and its own left. So
we break the field's (0.64, 0) into a piece along each. It is forward's 60° triangle with
k = 0.64: 0.64 × cos 60° = 0.32 forward, and -0.64 × sin 60° = -0.55 left. End to end, they
reach the same tip. Now the grid is the robot's.

:::{admonition} fig-e142-forward-split

:::

We do the same for (0, 0.16). It is left's triangle, its 60° at field y, with k = 0.16:
0.16 × sin 60° = 0.14 forward, and 0.16 × cos 60° = 0.08 left.
This picture is drawn 3 times as large, grid and all, so the pieces show.

we swap the middle two.

Add the forward pieces: 0.32 + 0.14 = 0.46. That is `robotForwardXSpeed`. Add the left
pieces: -0.55 + 0.08 = -0.47. That is `robotStrafeYSpeed`. The robot drives (0.46, -0.47) in its own
frame, and goes where the stick points on the field.
