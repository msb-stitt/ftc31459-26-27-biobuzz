# 035 what's happening: sticks into speeds

When the computer runs your program, it reads one line of code at a time. It moves words and
numbers around in its memory. It is like moving sticky notes around on your desk.

This page is about one line you wrote in [L030](l030.md), and why it has a minus sign.

```java
double leftSpeed = -leftStickY;
```

:::{admonition} fig-e035-numberline
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-numberline`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Start with a number line. Zero is in the middle. The numbers grow to the right. Numbers to the
right of zero are positive, with a + sign. Numbers to the left are negative, with a - sign.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-xy
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-xy`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Two number lines can cross at zero. The one that goes across is called x. The one that goes up is
called y. People almost always draw them this way: +x to the right, and +y up the page. Together
they are a **coordinate system**. It says which way each number points. The blue square is a clear
plastic sheet over the corner where x and y are both positive, with a line every 1.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-xyz
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-xyz`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

A third number line, z, sticks straight out of the sheet. To see it, lay the sheet flat on a table
and look from above and to the side, the way Onshape shows things. +z points up.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-flip
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-flip`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Now flip the sheet over, top to bottom. It turns on the x axis: the far edge comes up and over,
toward you.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-flipped
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-flipped`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

After the flip, +x still points the same way. But +y now points back toward you, and +z points
down. The sheet now lies on the other side of the x axis, over the gray squares. That is how you
can tell it was flipped. A gamepad stick uses the flipped sheet: its +x points right, and its +y
points back toward you, the driver.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-stick
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-stick`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

Here is the left stick from straight above, with you at the bottom. Pull the stick back and
`leftStickY` reads 1.0. Push it away and it reads -1.0. That is how most gamepads are made. It is
not a mistake in your code.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-robot
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-robot`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

The robot has a coordinate system of its own. +x points straight out of the robot's nose. That is
forward. +y points to the robot's left. Pedro Pathing, which drives the robot by itself in later
lessons, uses this one. This page only needs forward.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-speeds
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-speeds`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

A speed tells the wheels how fast to go, and which way. We like forward to be positive. A speed of
1.0 is full speed forward, 0.0 is stopped, and -1.0 is full speed backward. In tank drive,
`leftSpeed` is for the left wheels and `rightSpeed` is for the right wheels.

:::{admonition} fig-e035-switch
:class: pencil
Stand-in for `fig-e035-switch`, made by `book/tools/draw.py`.
:::

When you drive, pushing a stick away from you should drive the robot forward. Pushed away, the
stick reads -1.0. Its wheels should go full speed forward, 1.0. The stick's +y points back at you,
and the robot's forward points away from you, so the two numbers have opposite signs.

The minus sign switches from one coordinate system to the other. It turns -1.0 into 1.0, and 1.0
into -1.0:

```java
double leftSpeed = -leftStickY;
double rightSpeed = -rightStickY;
```

`leftStickY` is what the stick says. `leftSpeed` is how fast the wheels should go. They are numbers
in two different coordinate systems, so they get two different names.
